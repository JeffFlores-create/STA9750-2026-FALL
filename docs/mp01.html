load_netflix_data <- function(){
  # Note to students: Make sure you have installed the tidyverse and fs packages
  #                   before beginning this assignment. If you aren't sure,
  #                   simply run `library(tidyverse)` at your R Console and, if
  #                   you don't see an error message, `tidyverse` has been 
  #                   installed. (Same for `fs`) If you still need to install
  #                   either package, use the `install.packages` function as 
  #                   demonstrated in class.
library(tidyverse)
library(rvest)
library(readxl)
library(fs)
library(scales)
library(gt)
  data_dir <- path("data", "mp01")
  
  if(!dir_exists(data_dir)){
    dir_create(data_dir, showWarnings=FALSE, recurse=TRUE)
  }
  
  NETFLIX_URLS <- list(
    `2023H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-a-netflix-engagement-report",
    `2023H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2023",
    `2024H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2024",
    `2024H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2024",
    `2025H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2025",
    `2025H2` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-second-half-of-2025",
    `2026H1` = "https://about.netflix.com/news/what-we-watched-the-first-half-of-2026"
  )
  
  download_if_needed <- function(url, local){
    if(!file_exists(local)){
      download.file(url, local, quiet = TRUE, mode = "wb")
    }
    
    return(local)
  }
  
  NETFLIX_URLS |> 
    map(read_html) |> 
    map(html_element, xpath = "//a[contains(@href, '.xlsx')]") |> 
    map_chr(html_attr, "href") |> 
    enframe("period", "url") |> 
    mutate(path = path(data_dir, paste0(period, ".xlsx")), 
           path = map2(url, path, download_if_needed), 
           data = map(path, read_excel, skip=3, na="*"), 
           data = map(data, \(x) x |> mutate(`Release Date` = as.Date(`Release Date`)))) |>
    unnest(data) |> 
    select(-url, -path) |> 
    mutate(year=str_sub(period, 1, 4), 
           half=str_sub(period, start=6), 
           period_start = make_date(year, if_else(half==1, 1, 7), 1), 
           period_end = make_date(year, if_else(half==1, 7, 12), if_else(half==1, 30, 31))) |> 
    filter(!is.na(`Hours Viewed`)) |> 
    mutate(live_event = !is.na(Runtime) & str_detect(Runtime, "\\d+:\\d+\\*"), 
           clean_time = str_remove(Runtime, fixed("*")),
           Runtime = 60 * hour(hm(clean_time)) + minute(hm(clean_time))) |>
    select(-clean_time) |>
    mutate(Runtime = coalesce(Runtime, mean(Runtime, na.rm=TRUE)), 
           .by=Title, 
           Title = stringi::stri_trans_general(Title, "Latin-ASCII"),
           Title = str_remove(Title, " // .*"), 
           Title = str_remove(Title, "[^[:ascii:]].*"), 
           Title = str_trim(Title)) |>
    filter_out(Title == "") |>
    select(year, 
           period, 
           period_start, 
           period_end, 
           Title, 
           -half, 
           Runtime, 
           Views, 
           `Hours Viewed`, 
           `Release Date`,
           `Available Globally?`, 
           live_event) |>
    rename(title=Title,
           runtime=Runtime,
           views=Views,
           hours_viewed=`Hours Viewed`,
           release_date=`Release Date`,
           available_globally= `Available Globally?`,
           ) |> 
    mutate(
    year= as.integer(year), 
    available_globally = if_else(available_globally=="Yes",TRUE,FALSE)
    )
  
}

3. Most-Watched Titles by Period

To better understand Netflix’s viewing numbers, I identified the most watched title in each reporting period and calculated its proportion of total viewing hours for its respecive period. This allows us to see how much of Netflix’s viewership is driven by its biggest titles and whether that share changes over time.

Show Table Code

  summarise(total_hours = sum(hours_viewed, na.rm = TRUE),.groups = "drop")|>

8. Executive Memo

To: Netflix Leadership Team

From: Jeffrey Flores

Subject: Content Strategy Recommendations for 2027 and Beyond

With increasing competition in the streaming industry, Netflix must continue to find ways to attract viewers while keeping its existing audience engaged. After examining Netflix’s viewership data from 2023 through the first half of 2026, I believe the company should focus on three areas: balancing major original releases with consistently watched content, continuing to invest in TV shows, and continue to expand into live programming.

When analyzing the top 15 titles from Netflix’s catalog, I observed that high average vieweing hours didn’t necessarily mean consistent viewership. This could stem from a show having a positive word of mouth which caused it to reach such a high average view count but eventually those viewers went away, which would explain their lack of consistent viewership. Looking at the top 15 titles dataset, we saw that the Night Agent: Season 1 accumulated an average of 222.2 million viewing hours with a coefficient of variation of 119.5%. In comparison, Pablo Escobar, el patrón del mal: Season 1 averaged 136.2 million hours but had a much lower coefficient of variation of 16.8%. The Night Agent’s higher CV indicates greater fluctuations in viewership across all the periods. Pablo Escobar’s lower CV suggests steadier viewing hours. This suggests that consistent viewership can be valuable even when a title does not reach the same peaks as Netflix’s biggest hits. Netflix should continue to make and invest in productions that can maintain long-term audience engagement.

Moving onto the importance of TV shows, we saw they accounted for a much larger share of Netflix’s overall viewership. In the latest period, TV shows accounted for approximately 94.7% of total viewing hours, compared with 2.8% for movies. It’s important to note that this discrepancy can be explained by the fact that a season of a show typically has a longer runtime. Netflix should continue developing original TV series as this is where the crux of their viewership comes from. This doesn’t mean Netflix should stop producing movies, but instead put more thought into decisions on how its budget is allocated, as the data shows that TV series’s provides a greater overall contribution to Netflix’s total viewing hours than movies.

Finally, live programming has shown potential to provide Netflix with a great boost in viewers. Live events averaged approximately 1.53 million views, compared with 1.37 million for prerecorded content. These live events provide Netflix with a substantial number of viewers to their streaming service in a short time. It is a great way to attract new customers to the platform in the hopes of retaining them as long-time customers. Netflix should continue to expand into live programming, particularly through events which have demonstrated strong audience interest.

To conclude, for 2027 and beyond, I believe Netflix’s content strategy should focus on maintaining long-term engagement, further expansion into live programming, and invest in TV series (whether that be original or licensed TV shows). By combining these 3 strategies, Netflix can work on maintaining a competitive edge in the industry, attract and retain a new customer base, and keep its existing customers engaged.
